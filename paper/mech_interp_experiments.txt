Research Plan — Extended Paper
Does the Attention/MLP Division of Labour Scale to Domain Level?
Target: EMNLP 2026 | Prior work: ICLR 2026 submission
1 The Question
A transformer has two main computational components inside each layer: the attention sublayer
and the MLP sublayer. The mechanistic interpretability literature has established, at the level
of individual tokens and specific factual associations, that these two components play different
functional roles. Attention handles routing and contextual organisation — moving and aligning
information. MLPs function as key-value memories — storing and retrieving associations between
input patterns and output content. This picture has been built up carefully through work on feed-
forward layers as memory, factual knowledge localisation, and circuit-level analysis of individual
attention heads.
What has not been established is whether this same division holds at a qualitatively coarser level
of abstraction: entire semantic domains. When a model processes a physics abstract versus
an economics abstract, is the same organisational principle at work? Does attention route at the
domain level the same way it routes at the token level? Does MLP store domain-specific knowledge
the same way it stores individual facts?
The central question: Does the attention/MLP functional division that exists at the token
and fact level scale up coherently to domain-level processing — and if so, what does each
component actually contribute, and is that contribution localised or distributed across layers?
This is not a restatement of what is already known. The token-level results are all compatible
with a weak interpretation: that the attention/MLP division is simply an architectural artifact of
how gradients flow through different structures during training. A division that operates at the
level of individual tokens does not necessarily organise coherently at the level of semantic domains.
Whether it does is an open empirical question, and answering it constrains what kind of system a
trained transformer actually is.
2 Why This Question Matters
If the division does not scale to domain level — if both attention and MLP contributions are
equally and diffusely distributed across all layers when processing domain-specific text — then
the token-level functional specialisation is a fine-grained local phenomenon with no higher-level
organisational counterpart. The model is, at the domain level, essentially a diffuse function ap-
proximator. Mechanistic interpretability tools built on the token-level picture would not extend
upward in abstraction.
If the division does scale, and moreover if it is localised — meaning the routing function is
compressed into a small number of identifiable attention layers rather than distributed across all of
them — then transformers develop a hierarchical organisational principle. The same architectural
1
seam (attention vs. MLP) that organises computation at the token level is being used to organise
computation at the semantic domain level, and the routing function is not diffuse but structurally
concentrated. This changes the foundational picture of what a trained transformer is: not a diffuse
statistical machine, but a system with identifiable, reusable structural roles operating across levels
of abstraction. That is a meaningful constraint on how we think about interpretation, intervention,
and what mechanistic analysis can tell us.
The prior work (ICLR 2026 submission) found evidence for this picture but with three critical
weaknesses: the experiments measured component outputs rather than component contributions
to the residual stream, the causal experiment was technically flawed and uninterpretable, and the
dataset mixed surface-form-different corpora (code, medical text, finance) where separation could
be trivially explained by vocabulary. This work fixes all three.
3 Our Positioning
The existing literature establishes the attention/MLP division at the token and fact level. We ask
whether this division scales to domain-level processing, and if so, whether it is organised (localised
to specific layers) or diffuse. Our main contribution is to characterise the structure of this division
— not just that it exists, but where it lives and whether it causally controls domain-level behaviour.
We test this on arXiv abstracts across six academic disciplines: mathematics, physics, computer
science, statistics, quantitative biology, and economics. This dataset is the right choice precisely
because all six domains share the same surface form — all are formal academic abstracts with the
same genre structure. Any domain separation we observe in internal representations is therefore
genuinely semantic, not a vocabulary artifact. The cs/statistics boundary is intentionally included
as the hardest test case.
We study domain-level processing through a single unifying mathematical object — the domain
concept vector — which we define rigorously, and then use consistently across all three experi-
ments. Each experiment asks a different question, but all three speak to the same objects and all
three results reinforce the same claim. This is not a collection of loosely related analyses; it is a
single argument made from three angles.

4.3 The Simplex Test
Park et al. (2024) showed that when a model cleanly separates K categories, the K mean-shifted
concept vectors form a regular simplex in representation space — they are equally spaced in all
directions around the origin, like the vertices of an equilateral triangle (for K = 3) generalised to
higher dimensions.
For a regular K-simplex, the pairwise cosine similarity between any two vertices equals exactly
−1/(K − 1). For K = 6 domains, the ideal value is −1/5 = −0.2.

6.3 The Vocabulary Baseline
Early layers may show high separability simply because different domains use different vocabulary.
This would not be an interesting finding. We establish a token distribution baseline: compute
the FDR on raw unigram token distributions per domain (i.e., the embedding-weighted mean with
no forward pass). Any layer whose FDR does not exceed this baseline is showing separability that
is purely vocabulary-driven. We report which layers exceed the baseline, and only those layers are
considered meaningful.

7 Experiment 2: Fine-Tuning Analysis
Question: When you fine-tune the model on a specific domain, where do the weight updates
go? Are the MLP updates larger, and more importantly, are they domain-specific rather than just
generically large?
This experiment tests the storage hypothesis from the adaptation side. The key improvement over
the prior work: we do not just measure how much weights change. We measure whether MLP
weight changes are aligned with the domain concept vectors — i.e., whether the updates specifically
modify the directions in weight space that carry domain identity.

Fine-tune six separate models, one per domain, from the same Llama-3.1-8B base checkpoint using
LoRA (rank 16, α = 32, applied to Wq, Wv, Wup, Wdown at all layers). Three epochs on each
domain’s 3,500 training abstracts.

8 Experiment 3: Causal Activation Patching
Question: Is the domain signal in attention causally responsible for domain-directed behaviour?
And if so, does it propagate through MLP layers downstream, or does it ride passively through the
stream?
The first two experiments are observational and adaptive. This experiment is causal. It asks: if
you surgically change the domain routing signal in attention at a specific layer, does the model’s
subsequent computation change in a directed, domain-consistent way?

8.3 What Propagation Tells You
There are three possible outcomes, all interpretable:
Signal amplifies through downstream MLP layers. The shift score sℓ grows specifically
at the MLP sublayers immediately following the patch. This is the clearest confirmation of the
routing/execution picture: attention at ℓ∗ injected a domain signal, and downstream MLP layers
responded to it by amplifying domain-B-consistent computation. The routing decision at attention
triggers execution in MLP.
Signal persists but does not amplify. sℓ stays roughly constant after the patch. The domain-B
signal rides along in the stream without anything actively responding to it. This is a weaker version
of the routing hypothesis: attention controls what is in the stream, but MLP does not specially act
on domain signals at this level of abstraction. Still interpretable and publishable.
Signal decays. sℓ decreases after the patch — downstream layers actively pull the stream back
toward domain-A processing. This would mean domain identity is maintained redundantly across
multiple layers, not localised at the hotspots. This would challenge the localisation claim and is
the most important failure mode to watch for.

9 What the Experiments Say Together
Each experiment asks a different question, all about the same objects:
1. Concept vectors + separability: Do attention contributions at specific layers carry dispro-
portionate domain signal, and is it geometrically organised? (Observational)
2. Fine-tuning: Do weight updates during domain-specific training modify the directions in MLP
weight space that correspond to domain concept vectors? (Adaptive)
3. Causal patching: Does surgically injecting a domain concept direction into attention at
hotspot layers cause directed domain shifts that propagate through downstream MLP? (Causal)
If all three work, the argument is:
• Attention contributes disproportionate, localised, geometrically-organised domain signal at spe-
cific layers (Experiment 1).
• MLP weight updates are larger, more domain-specific, and specifically aligned with domain
concept vectors (Experiment 2).
• Patching the domain direction in attention at those specific layers causes directed domain shifts
that are amplified by downstream MLP layers (Experiment 3).
Together: the attention/MLP functional division scales to domain level. Routing is compressed
into identifiable attention layers. Execution happens in MLP. The organisation is not diffuse but
structural.
